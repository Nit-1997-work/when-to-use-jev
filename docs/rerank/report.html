Experiment 2 · Run main-20261002

Jev vs Gemini Flash-Lite for product search re-ranking

Can TypeSafe's Jev replace a typical LLM re-ranker, where Gemini Flash-Lite ranks a shortlist of products in one structured-output call? This document covers the experiment end to end: the question, the data, the ten systems, how they were measured, and what the full run on 1,700 held-out Amazon searches showed.

Dataset: Amazon ESCI (KDD Cup 2022), English, reduced 1,000 main + 300 no-match + 100 x 3 consistency + 100 reversed searches Jev: jev-1.13.0 Gemini: 3.5 Flash-Lite, 3.7 Flash Run date: 2026-10-02 (UTC) Code: commit f00502f

Summary

On re-ranking, Jev beats the production-style baseline (gemini-listwise) on quality, latency, and cost all at once. The best Jev designs rank as well as Gemini Flash, the larger model, at about one twentieth of its p95 latency and one sixth to one thirty-fifth of its cost. Where Jev is weaker is deciding that nothing on the list matches: its tuned thresholds wrongly abstain on a fifth to a third of searches that do have a match.

The five things to take away

  1. Quality: three of four Jev designs beat gemini-listwise on Exact@1 with statistical significance, and two on NDCG@3. None is worse.
  2. Speed and cost: every single-call Jev design is 3.6x to 5x faster at p95 and 2.4x to 14.6x cheaper.
  3. Model, not approach: given the same per-candidate labelling task, Jev beats Gemini by about 0.07 NDCG@3. Gemini needs to see the whole list to compete; Jev does not.
  4. Order robustness: Jev's top product survives reversing the candidate order in 54% to 75% of searches, against 14% to 43% for Gemini.
  5. Weak spot: abstention. F1 is about the same for both (49% to 54%), but Jev trades it for many more false "nothing matches" calls (22% to 35%, vs 10% to 15%). Thresholds tuned on 150 practice searches did not carry over.

1. Question and scope

Product search is usually two steps. A fast retriever (keyword, embedding, or both) returns a shortlist of a few dozen products. A small LLM then re-ranks that shortlist in one structured-output call, and flags when nothing on it really matches. The application acts on the first result and shows the next few as alternatives, so top-1 matters most, then the top 3.

This experiment tests only the second step, and asks how Jev compares with that typical setup on:

Primary
Ranking quality
Is the best product first, and are the top 3 good?
Primary
Latency
Time to re-rank one shortlist, p50 / p95 / p99.
Primary
Cost
Dollars to re-rank one shortlist, at list price.

Secondary questions: abstention (noticing that nothing is an exact match), consistency (same ranking on a repeat and on a reversed list), and approach vs model (how much of a difference comes from Jev itself, and how much from scoring candidates separately rather than ranking the whole list).

In scope

  • Re-ranking a fixed shortlist of real products for a short query
  • Detecting that no candidate is an exact match
  • Quality, latency, cost, consistency

Out of scope

  • Retrieval, query rewriting, category tagging
  • Personalization and session context
  • Price-aware ranking (no prices in the data; numbers belong in code)
  • Writing the "couldn't find it" message (generation stays with an LLM)

Why Jev might fit, and where it might not

Re-ranking is a set of short judgments ("how well does this product match this query?"), which is the narrow, structured decision Jev is built for. Jev's documented weak spots were designed around up front:

Jev weak spotWhere it bites in re-rankingMitigation in this design
Literal reading"pineapple" vs pineapple juice; "gluten-free bread" vs plain breadExact/substitute boundary written into the criteria, with grocery examples
IndirectionA question naming one candidate must find it in a long listCandidates keyed by short id (candidates.c7); a one-candidate-per-request variant is also tested
Large, irrelevant stateLong product descriptionsTitle, brand, and color only
Numbers"under $5", "family size"Out of scope; keep in code
GenerationThe friendly "couldn't find white rum" messageNot measured; a hybrid would call an LLM only when Jev abstains

3. Dataset and splits

Amazon Shopping Queries Dataset (ESCI), KDD Cup 2022, Apache-2.0. Real, difficult Amazon queries, each with a shortlist of products, and every query-product pair labelled by a human as EExact, SSubstitute, CComplement, or IIrrelevant. The reduced "Task 1" English version has 29,844 queries and 601,354 judgments, with a median of 16 candidates per query.

It matches the job exactly: a query, a real shortlist, and graded labels that line up with a re-ranker's decisions (gluten-free vs plain bread is E vs S; rum extract for "rum" is C).

Splits

Built deterministically, by search, with no search in two splits. Only searches with 8 to 40 candidates are used (drops 2.2% of test searches). Tuning splits come from ESCI train; every reported split comes from ESCI test.

SplitSearchesBuilt fromUse
practice100ESCI trainTune labels, prompts, criteria. Never reported.
practice-nomatch50ESCI train, Exact candidates removedTune Jev abstention thresholds.
main1,000ESCI test: 171 reviewed grocery + 829 randomHeadline quality, latency, cost; false abstentions.
nomatch300ESCI test, Exact candidates removedAbstention: nothing on the list is an exact match.
consistency100ESCI testRun 3 times: same ranking on a repeat?
reversed100Same 100 searches, order reversed, ids reassignedRun once: does the top product depend on order?
warmup20ESCI trainDiscarded warm-up.

How candidates are presented

  • Every labelled candidate for the search, in a seeded per-search shuffle, with ids c1...cN in presented order. ESCI has no original search-engine order, and a random order keeps systems from inheriting a hint.
  • Every system sees the same candidates in the same order under the same ids.
  • Product text is title (capped at 300 characters), brand, and color.

No-match searches

Only 1 of 29,844 reduced English searches has no Exact candidate, so every no-match search is constructed: take a test search, remove every Exact candidate, keep it if at least 5 remain. This mirrors a catalog that does not carry the item but carries near alternatives. No-match searches are scored on abstention only, since NDCG is undefined without an Exact candidate.

One set of label definitions for everyone

All systems see the same four definitions from data/rerank/labels.yaml, written once from ESCI's guidelines for a shopper's search and frozen before the main run. Gemini gets them as JSON in its prompt; Jev gets the same objects as Score criteria, their exact-match wording as Noul criteria, and the descriptions as Choice text.

LevelMeansExample
EExactThe thing searched for, meeting every stated requirement (brand, flavor, form, size, "without X")."shredded cheddar cheese" → a bag of shredded cheddar
SSubstituteSame kind of thing, misses at least one stated requirement; could be bought instead."gluten-free bread" → regular whole wheat bread
CComplementNot what was searched for, but used with it."lavazza coffee machine" → Lavazza coffee pods

The main Jev request

from typesafe_sdk import Score

state = {"query": search.query,
         "candidates": {"c1": {"title": ..., "brand": ..., "color": ...}, ...}}
questions = {
    cid: Score(
        instructions=f"{labels.instruction} How well does the product `candidates.{cid}` match `query`?",
        criteria=[irrelevant, complement, substitute, exact],  # each {"means", "not", "example"}
    )
    for cid in state["candidates"]
}
response = await client.system_one(state, questions)
# response.answers[cid].probabilities → {0: p_irrelevant, 1: p_complement, 2: p_substitute, 3: p_exact}
# rank by expected gain: sum(p_level * KDD gain), gains E 1.0, S 0.1, C 0.01, I 0

5. Metrics and fairness rules

Ranking quality (on main)

ESCI labels become gains with the KDD Cup 2022 values: Exact 1.0, Substitute 0.1, Complement 0.01, Irrelevant 0. Each step is ten times the one below, so any Exact in the top 3 outweighs three Substitutes there. Scores come only from the human labels; no model judges another model.

MetricWhy it matters
NDCG@3 (headline)The product the app acts on and the alternatives shown beside it
Exact@1Is the product the app acts on an exact match? random already gets about 42%.
Usable@1Is the top product at least Exact or Substitute?
MRR of first ExactHow far down the first right answer is
NDCG@10Overall list quality
NDCG@3 gentleGains E 1, S 0.5, C 0.1, I 0: does the winner depend on how much a Substitute is worth?

Statistics: 95% bootstrap confidence intervals over searches. Against gemini-listwise: a paired permutation test on the NDCG@3 difference, and McNemar's exact test on Exact@1.

Latency

  • Client wall-clock per search, request sent to parsed ordering; for per-candidate systems, the whole parallel fan-out.
  • One clean attempt timed: SDK retries off; runner retries and backoff recorded separately.
  • Gateway cache bypassed; cache hits flagged and excluded.

Cost

  • Tokens from every response, summed per search, including Gemini reasoning tokens, at list price on the run date.
  • Blocked responses are billed and included.

Abstention

  • Precision, recall, F1 over main + nomatch; false-abstention rate on main.

Consistency

  • Top-1 agreement and Kendall's tau across 3 repeats; top-1 agreement with the order reversed.

Also reported

  • 4-way label accuracy and confusion, calibration of Jev's P(exact), a Jev-then-Gemini cascade, and reliability counts.

Fairness rules

  • Same query, candidates, order, ids, and product fields for every system.
  • Same frozen label definitions for every system.
  • Zero-shot only. Jev cannot be fine-tuned, so no labelled examples in any prompt.
  • Prompts, questions, and thresholds changed only on practice splits; thresholds frozen with their hash recorded.
  • Gemini at temperature 0, provider default thinking and safety settings.
  • Blocked searches and API errors are excluded from quality metrics and reported separately.

Why jev-score misses the 5x cost bar Jev bills input tokens per question, and the shared state (query plus every candidate) is counted again for each question. A search with N candidates therefore costs roughly N times the state, plus each question's criteria. jev-score uses about 10,900 input tokens per search against 2,200 for gemini-listwise; Jev's per-token price is 7x lower, which nets out to 2.4x cheaper. jev-noul-batch carries much shorter criteria (match / no-match) and lands at 5.5x; jev-choice asks one question per search and lands at 14.6x.
Is the latency gap the gateway? No. Gemini calls go through a LiteLLM-based gateway, but its measured overhead on gemini-listwise was a median of 3.4 ms against a median of about 920 ms upstream. Gemini Flash's long tail (p99 11 s) also explains why it took 68 minutes for main while every Jev system finished it in 7 or less.

11a. Approach vs model

The design pairs systems that do the same task with different models, to separate "Jev is better at this" from "scoring candidates separately is better".

Same task, different model (NDCG@3 on main)
Each pair asks the two models the same question shape.
The model matters On the same per-candidate labelling task, Jev beats Gemini Flash-Lite by 0.066 NDCG@3 (one call per search) and 0.074 (one call per candidate). The gap is larger than any difference between Jev designs.
Gemini needs the whole list; Jev does not Gemini's quality drops by 0.045 when it labels instead of ranks, and by 0.068 when it sees one candidate at a time. Jev loses only 0.012 to 0.015 going from batch to one-candidate-per-request. Listwise ranking lets Gemini compare candidates against each other; Jev's per-candidate probabilities already carry that information.

A likely reason, inferred from the label-quality results below: Jev and Gemini are about equally accurate at picking the single right label (55% to 58%), but Jev returns a probability for every level. Ranking by expected gain separates candidates within a label, while a Gemini label leaves ties that fall back to presented order.

Abstention is the one area where Jev is not a drop-in replacement F1 is about the same for every system (49% to 54%), so nobody does this well. But the frozen Jev thresholds sit at a different point: they catch more true no-match searches and also say "nothing matches" on 22% to 35% of searches that have a match, 1.5x to 3.5x the Gemini rate. In a shopping app a false "couldn't find it" is usually worse than a missed one. The thresholds were tuned for F1 on only 150 practice searches (F1 66% to 68% there), and that did not carry over to test data.

Because Jev returns a confidence, the operating point is a dial, not a fixed behaviour; a production setting would choose a threshold to cap false abstentions (for example at Gemini's 10% to 15%) on a larger tuning set, and accept the lower recall. That trade-off was not measured here and is a recommended follow-up.

17. Limitations and threats to validity

Data

  • Amazon's general catalog, not a grocery catalog; the grocery slice is a keyword filter plus review (171 searches).
  • Every no-match search is constructed by removing Exact candidates.
  • Labels are noisy; nearly identical products sometimes carry different labels.
  • No prices, no session context, no original search order.
  • Public data: either model may have seen it in training, which can inflate absolute scores (but not obviously favour one side).

Run

  • One run, from one laptop on home Wi-Fi; systems ran concurrently and shared the machine and network. Absolute latencies will differ in a data center; the ratios are the useful part.
  • Abstention thresholds tuned on 150 searches; consistency and reversed splits are 100 searches each.
  • Gemini at temperature 0 and default thinking; no prompt engineering beyond the shared definitions, and no few-shot examples for either side.
  • Product text is title, brand, color only; descriptions might help either system.

Open items carried from the design

  • The source and date for Jev's price per million input tokens ($0.042 used here) still need confirming.
  • Whether TypeSafe's Master Customer Agreement allows publishing benchmark results is unresolved; treat this document as internal until it is.

18. Recommendations

For a shopping re-ranker

  1. Use jev-noul-batch as the default re-ranker. It meets every pre-registered bar with significant quality gains, and costs about $197 per million searches.
  2. Consider jev-choice when cost and latency dominate: comparable Exact@1, highest repeat consistency of any Jev design, $74 per million. Its NDCG@10 is lower (0.773), so prefer it when only the top result is shown.
  3. Keep the "nothing matches" decision separate. Re-tune the abstention threshold on a larger, catalog-specific set, with a cap on false abstentions rather than maximum F1; then call an LLM to write the message only when Jev abstains.
  4. Plan a fallback for searches the provider filter would block if any LLM stays in the path.

Follow-up experiments

  • Abstention operating curve: false-abstention rate vs recall for each Jev system, tuned on a larger set.
  • Shorter Score criteria for jev-score to bring its cost under the 5x bar while keeping its quality.
  • A labelled sample from the target catalog, to confirm the result on real grocery naming.
  • A cascade with Gemini Flash (not Flash-Lite) on the hard slice only.
  • Latency from a data-center client with systems run one after another.
